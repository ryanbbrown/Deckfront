Working spec for the next generation of Deckfront. This is a scratch document for iteration, not a committed ruleset.

The previous generation — territory control with recruiting, supply centers, and income — is a different game and is being retired. Nothing in this document should be read as a variant of it.

Design intent

The deck stays relevant for the entire game because every card pays out each time it is drawn. Cards are never consumed, never attached, and never converted into one-time board effects. Improving deck density and draw keeps mattering on the last turn as much as the first.

What version 1 has to answer

Is it actually viable to build meaningfully different units — a very fast soldier, a very long-ranged archer, a hard-hitting brawler — or does the upgrade economy collapse into one correct build?

Everything else is subordinate to that question. Version 1 is deliberately thin: no interaction cards, no terrain manipulation, no conditional cards. A dozen or so cards total. It will feel sparse to play. That is correct for a tuning experiment, because a wider pool would make it impossible to tell whether a build won on its merits or on card draw.

Why HP went up

HP does most of the differentiating work, since it is the one stat neither type can upgrade and nothing in version 1 restores it. The gap is two points rather than one, because a one-point difference is inside the noise of a single exchange.

The absolute values are set by what happens at the end of the game, not the start. Attack climbs to 4 or 5 on a specialist, and any unit that dies to a single hit stops being a piece and becomes a liability you cannot position. At 3 HP an archer dies to one attack from any moderately upgraded unit, which deletes the entire late-game archer decision space. At 4 it survives one hit from attack 3.

The cost is a slower opening: six hits to kill a soldier at attack 1. That is acceptable, because the armies spend the first two or three turns crossing the board anyway and neither deck can do anything with a kill yet. If run 1 shows the endgame resolving in a single exchange, HP is the first dial, not attack.

Why the soldier keeps a movement edge

The gap is only one point, but it cannot go to zero. Consider an archer at range 2 facing a soldier at range 1, both activating move–attack–move. The archer shoots, then retreats its full movement; the soldier must close to adjacent to strike at all.

With movement 4 against 3, the gap shrinks by one hex per round, so the soldier eventually arrives. With movement equal, the gap never closes: the archer attacks every round and the soldier never attacks once. Equal movement is not a mild imbalance, it is a hard lock.

The consequence carries into the upgrade economy. If an archer buys movement up to 4, the soldier must reach 5 to keep closing. That is the reason archer movement is sold on a card that does not cycle — see Known tensions.

Every unit is a distinct, persistent object with its own stats. Two soldiers that started identical will diverge as you upgrade them.

Army setup

Each player has exactly five units and chooses the distribution between soldiers and archers at setup.

Core constraint

There is no recruiting, no reinforcement, and no revival. A dead unit is gone for the rest of the game, and every upgrade invested in it is gone with it.

Players also draft starting cards before turn one, as in the previous generation. See The market for the proposed draft.

Because the two card pools are fully disjoint, the setup distribution is not just a board decision — it determines which half of the supply is useful to you for the rest of the game. A three–two split has to buy from both pools and will be less efficient at both.

The board Settled

A tall, narrow hex map with pointy-top hexes. One player deploys along the top edge, the other along the bottom.

17 rows. Widths alternate: the nine odd-numbered rows are 9 hexes wide, the eight even-numbered rows are 10 and sit half a hex further out on each side. Row 1 and row 17 are the home rows. Total playable area is 161 hexes before walls.

Home rows Wall R · Range A · Attack M · Movement

The key point positions and the 180° rotation are fixed. The wall layout shown is one illustrative arrangement, not the chosen one — nine seed hexes in the top half, rotated into the bottom.

Symmetry

The board has 180° rotational symmetry about the centre hex. Top-left lands in bottom-right. Terrain is authored for one half and rotated into the other, so whatever one player faces on the way in, the other faces the same shapes turned around.

Rotation rather than reflection is what lets the two flanks differ. Under a top-to-bottom mirror, the left wing and the right wing are the same wing seen twice, and any plan for one is the plan for the other. Under rotation each player's left is the other player's right, so the wings stay distinct without either player owning the better one.

What it costs is the neutrality of the two outer key points. The attack point sits on the centre hex, which rotation fixes, so it is genuinely equidistant. The range and movement points swap under rotation: both are eight rows from either home row, so raw distance is equal, but the terrain on the way is not. The walls one player crosses to reach range are the walls the other crosses to reach movement.

That is only a real edge if one of those two stats is worth more than the other in a given matchup, and it shrinks the sparser the terrain near the middle rows is. Keeping the approaches to the outer key points relatively clear is the cheap mitigation, and it costs nothing else in the design.

Walls

Walls occupy hexes. They block movement and they block line of sight. Nothing crazy: 16–20 wall hexes total, arranged in segments of length 2–4, rotated into the far half. Home rows and key point hexes stay clear.

Segments should run at angles, not just along rows. A horizontal bar only ever blocks an advance; a diagonal one blocks an advance from one side while opening a lane along its face, so it makes the two flanks approach it differently. Diagonals also do more work for line of sight, since a sightline down the board crosses them at an angle rather than meeting a flat edge.

A hex holds at most one unit. There are no supply centers, no home bases, no board income, and no board-side money. Apart from the key points, the map's only job is to create the geometry that positioning decisions happen inside.

Key points

Three hexes on the middle row grant free upgrades. They exist because nothing else in version 1 pulls the armies into contact — with supply centers gone and interaction cards deferred, the win condition alone is a very slow magnet.

Rule

If one of your units occupies a key point at the start of your turn, that unit immediately gains +1 to the point's stat. No symbols are spent and no threshold is paid.

The three points cover attack, movement, and range. The attack point sits at the true center hex, because it is the most universally wanted upgrade and should be the most contested; the other two sit on the flanks.

Timing is what makes them dangerous. Occupation is checked at the start of your turn, so the unit has to survive a full enemy turn standing on an exposed hex in the middle of the board to collect anything. You cannot walk onto a point and cash it in the same turn.

Key point upgrades count against the +1 per stat, per unit, per turn cap. A unit that takes +1 attack from the attack point cannot also buy attack with symbols that turn.

The range point is dead to a soldier — soldiers cannot upgrade range at all. It is still worth taking with one: standing on a point denies it, and denial is a real use even when the upgrade is not.

Two variants to test

Version 1 default

The occupying unit is upgraded

The unit standing on the point is the unit that gets stronger. Because a unit occupies exactly one hex, this caps key point gain at +1 per unit per turn.

It also forces the beneficiary to be the unit in danger, which produces escort play: park the archer on the range point and screen it with soldiers.

Fallback if too weak

Upgrade any unit anywhere

Holding a point grants a free upgrade usable on any unit you control, wherever it is.

Much stronger, and probably too strong: hold all three with expendable units and funnel +3 per turn into one unit sitting safely at the back, which is legal under the per-stat cap since they are three different stats. It also breaks the risk/reward loop, because the thing getting stronger is no longer the thing at risk.

Its one genuine advantage is that soldier-heavy armies could use the range point.

Start restrictive. The permissive version is a tuning lever to reach for only if the points turn out too weak to be worth walking to.

Why this is the archer's route to attack

A straight upgrade ignores the threshold rule, so a point is worth most to whoever is furthest up a curve or locked out of one. The archer is both: its attack cards are deliberately the worst rate in the game, so the attack point is the only cheap attack an archer will ever see.

That is the intended shape. An archer army that wants to kill anything has to come to the center and hold it, which is exactly the behaviour the points exist to produce.

Turn structure

Players alternate full turns.

  1. Trash. Optionally trash one card from hand. It is not played, and its money and symbols are lost for the turn.
  2. Play. Play every card drawn. Cards produce money and upgrade symbols.
  3. Buy. Spend money on cards from the supply.
  4. Key points. Each unit that started the turn on a key point takes its free upgrade.
  5. Upgrade. Spend symbols on permanent stat raises.
  6. Activate. Activate each of your units, one at a time, in any order.
  7. Check for a winner.

All upgrades resolve before any unit activates. Upgrades and activations are never interleaved, so a unit fights the whole turn with its final stats and you allocate symbols knowing the board you are allocating them for.

Key points resolve before symbol spending so you know what you already have before deciding where the symbols go.

Symbols and money do not carry between turns.

Deck

Core constraint

There are no actions. You play every card you draw. Draw is therefore the only engine axis and has to be priced expensively to compensate.

This means Village and similar action-splitting cards have no function and do not exist.

Cards are never consumed by producing symbols. A card that gives one attack symbol gives one attack symbol every single time it is drawn, forever.

Trashing is not a card

There is no trasher in the supply. Instead every player may trash one card from hand at the start of every turn, always, for free.

This is the only way to raise deck quality, which makes it load-bearing rather than optional — there are no card-acquisition engines here to out-scale a bloated deck. Trashing a card costs you its output for that turn, so the cards worth trashing are almost entirely the weak early buys you are trying to get rid of anyway.

Card shapes

Every card in version 1 is one of four shapes. The concrete list is in The market.

  • Money. Conventional treasure.
  • Draw. Expensive, produces no symbols. The engine axis.
  • Cycling symbol card. Draws a card and produces one symbol. Replaces itself, so it never clogs.
  • Dense symbol card. Produces two symbols and does not replace itself.

The two symbol shapes trade against each other over the arc of a game rather than dominating one another. In a deck you cannot fully draw, the cycling card is better because it costs you nothing to hold. In a deck you can fully draw, the dense card is better because every slot pays double and drawing is no longer scarce. Early decks want cycling; finished engines want density.

Core constraint

The two card pools are completely disjoint. Every symbol card is either a soldier card or an archer card. No card in version 1 works on both types, and there are no joint cards covering two stats.

The pools are the primary tool for differentiating the two types, and they are shaped by one structural rule rather than by scattered pricing:

Pool shape

Cycling cards exist only in a type's native lanes. Off-lane symbols are always sold on a card that does not replace itself.

  • Soldier — native in attack and movement. No soldier range card exists at all, so "soldiers cannot upgrade range" is enforced by the pool rather than by an exception.
  • Archer — native in range only. Attack and movement are off-lane, so an archer that wants either has to permanently clog its deck to get it.

This puts the anti-convergence pressure on deck structure instead of on price. A price penalty is paid once; a density penalty is paid every shuffle for the rest of the game, and clogging is the thing that actually kills engines. An archer can buy attack, and it will still be a worse attacker than a soldier ten turns later, because it spent the intervening turns drawing fewer cards.

So the archer's identity is that it reaches range no other unit can, and pays in engine speed to become anything else. The soldier's identity is that it climbs attack far faster than an archer ever will, and can never solve a problem at distance.

The cost is deck fragmentation: a mixed army buys from two pools and dilutes both. That is intentional, and it is the pressure that makes the setup split a real decision rather than a default 3–2.

Four piles per type, plus four shared. Soldiers get two native lanes because they only have two stats to raise; archers get one native lane and two off-lane piles because they have three. The pile counts come out even without being contrived.

The off-lane cards are the cycling card minus the draw

Bodkin and Skirmish Order are deliberately legible as a penalty: same cost as a native cycling card, same one symbol, and they simply do not draw. There is no arithmetic to do — an archer looking at Bodkin against Ranging can see it is paying 4 for the worse half of the same card.

That rate is bad enough that Bodkin may well never be bought, which is fine and possibly correct. The attack key point exists as the archer's real answer, and if it turns out archers need a purchasable attack lane at all, Bodkin's cost is the cheapest thing in this document to change.

Money and draw

Copper, Silver, Gold, and Muster are the only cards both armies buy, and they are the entire shared spine of the game. There are no victory cards, so nothing in any deck is dead.

One buy per turn. With the free trash, that caps deck growth at one net card per turn and lets a player hold deck size flat while raising quality — which is the whole shape of the economy here, since there is no way to gain extra buys.

Muster produces no symbols, which is its only brake. Every copy is a slot not producing symbols, and once a deck draws itself completely, further copies do nothing at all. It self-limits rather than needing a cost wall.

Draft

Proposed draft

Before turn one, each player takes up to three cards from the market with a total printed cost of at most 8. Choices are made simultaneously and revealed together. Your starting deck is 7 Copper plus whatever you drafted.

Eight coin buys two cycling cards, or one dense card and a Silver, or a single Muster with change. That is enough to declare a build and not enough to skip the opening.

Taking fewer cards is a real option rather than a wasted budget, because a smaller deck is a denser one: seven Copper and one Ranging draws that Ranging more often than seven Copper and two of them draws either. The draft is therefore a genuine decision about deck size, not just a shopping list.

Simultaneous and blind, so the draft is a declaration rather than a counter-pick. Piles hold ten copies, so both players wanting the same card is not a problem in the draft — though it becomes one later.

Pile sizes

Ten copies of each non-treasure pile; treasures effectively unlimited.

This is no longer an end-game trigger, but it is not cosmetic either. Across a full game the two players make on the order of forty buys between them, so a pile of ten is a real ceiling on a mono-lane strategy and a real opportunity to deny a mirror one. It is the only place in version 1 where the two decks touch each other at all.

What the economy has to produce

The number that matters

The binding constraint is peak symbols of a single type in a single turn, not symbols in total. Reaching range 6 requires one turn that produces six range symbols. Nothing carries over, so twelve range symbols spread across two turns buys strictly less than six in one.

That single fact drives every card decision. Density in one lane beats breadth across lanes, and the whole point of the engine is to raise the size of your best turn rather than your average one.

It also sets up the tall-versus-wide choice cleanly. Six range symbols will raise two archers from 2 to 3, or one archer from 5 to 6. Both spend every symbol, so neither is wasteful — the difference is that wide is available from turn one and tall is gated behind ever-larger single turns. Tall wins fights; wide is what you settle for while your deck is still small.

The costs above are aimed at roughly this curve:

If the curve comes out much faster, the game is decided before the board matters. Much slower and both armies fight the whole game at attack 1, which is the same as having no deck at all.

Upgrades

Upgrades are permanent, apply to one specific unit, and are lost when that unit dies.

The threshold rule

Raising a stat to value V costs V symbols of that type, spent in a single turn.

An archer at 1 attack pays 2 symbols to reach 2. A soldier already at 2 attack pays 3 to reach 3. Symbols do not accumulate across turns; anything unspent is lost.

A given stat on a given unit can only be raised by 1 per turn, from any source. Stacking 5 attack symbols on one archer still only raises it from 1 to 2, and the surplus is wasted.

Within one turn you may pay for as many separate raises as your symbols allow, as long as each is a different stat or a different unit. Four attack symbols will raise attack from 1 to 2 on two different units.

Upgradeable stats are attack, movement, and range.

Settled

HP does not appear in the upgrade system at all. There is no max HP raise, no healing, and no regeneration in version 1 — not deferred within the rules, simply absent.

The reason is that max HP is incoherent without healing: raising a ceiling you can never climb back to does nothing for a damaged unit. Rather than add a second mechanic to make the first one work, version 1 leaves both out and sets starting HP high enough to carry the whole job. If units prove too fragile, the answer is a bigger number on the unit card, not a new card in the market.

Anti-convergence

Soldiers cannot upgrade range. This is the one hard type constraint, and it is what stops the two unit types from collapsing into one.

There is no mirror constraint on the soldier side, and none is needed. Nothing about an archer buying attack or movement makes it a soldier — it still has three HP and it still paid far more than a soldier would have. Pricing, not prohibition, is the right tool in that direction.

Combat

A unit may attack once per activation.

Activation

Move, attack, move — drawing on a single shared movement budget. A soldier with movement 4 can advance 2, attack, and withdraw 2. It cannot move 4, attack, and then move 4 again.

This makes hit-and-run real while keeping it a genuine tradeoff: retreating far means approaching short.

An attack deals damage equal to the attacker's attack value. Ranged attacks additionally require line of sight: an unobstructed straight line from the center of the attacker's hex to the center of the target's hex.

Precise tiebreak

The line is blocked if it passes through the interior of a wall hex. Grazing a hex edge or touching a vertex does not block.

Stated this precisely because on a pointy-top board the vertical axis is the main axis of advance, and a straight vertical line runs through hex vertices rather than hex bodies. The grazing case will therefore come up constantly, not occasionally.

Damage persists between turns. There is no healing in version 1, so all damage is permanent and every unit only ever gets closer to death.

A unit at 0 HP is removed from the game permanently.

Winning

Win condition

Eliminate all five enemy units.

Because there are no reinforcements, you cannot win passively — the win condition is defined entirely in terms of the opponent's army, so at some point you have to go and fight.

Clock

The game also ends at a turn cap. If nobody has been eliminated by then, the winner is decided by most units remaining, then by total remaining HP.

The tiebreak is a rule. The cap value is not — it is a setting on whatever is running the game, chosen per batch and generous enough that only a genuine stalemate reaches it. A game that ends on the cap has told us something went wrong, so the number should be tuned to make that signal reliable rather than to shape play.

The cap replaces the old supply-pile end trigger, which no longer fits: with no actions and no victory cards, pile depletion tracks how much money got spent rather than how long the game has gone on, and it can be avoided outright by a player who simply stops buying.

Known tensions Watch in run 1

These are the things most likely to break. They should be watched in the first runs rather than discovered later.

The cost curve fights specialization

Because a raise costs the value being reached, taking one stat from base B up by k steps costs roughly quadratically in k. An archer going from range 2 to range 5 pays 3 + 4 + 5 = 12 symbols. Meanwhile that same archer can raise its low stats cheaply. So the arithmetic quietly pushes every unit toward being well-rounded, which is the opposite of the stated design goal.

Two release valves, in order of preference. First, symbol density: a native lane sells two symbols per card and an off lane sells one, so the specialist's expensive raises and the generalist's cheap ones cost a similar number of card slots even though they cost very different numbers of symbols. Second, the key points, which hand out raises at a flat cost and are therefore worth most to whoever is furthest up a curve. If the first runs come back with everyone building identical generalists, those are the dials to turn — not the threshold rule.

Nothing caps the cycling engine

Removing actions removes the only thing that normally limits how many self-replacing cards a deck can hold. A deck that is mostly cycling symbol cards draws through itself, and a deck that is entirely cycling cards plus Muster draws every card every turn. Expected cards seen is roughly 5 / (1 − p) where p is the fraction of the deck that replaces itself, so it goes vertical somewhere past three quarters.

Two things hold it down, and both are soft. Money does not cycle, so keeping enough coin to buy anything caps p. And the threshold rule is a genuine sink, since a huge turn is still only +1 per stat per unit. But if a player reaches full-deck draw by turn eight, they have effectively skipped the middle of the game. Watch how early the first full draw happens; if it is before turn twelve, cycling cards are too cheap.

Convergence on archer movement

Archers start at movement 3, one below the soldier, so their movement upgrades are the cheapest raises on the board by threshold. Left alone, archers buy movement to 4, the soldier's closing advantage vanishes, and the archer becomes strictly better than a soldier at everything except HP.

What holds this shut is that Skirmish Order does not cycle, so every point of archer movement is bought with permanent engine speed. That is a real cost but it is still only a cost, and a player willing to pay it can get there. Watch for the soldier count dropping to zero across a batch — that is the signal, and it arrives before anything else does.

Turtling

This version has no supply centers and no interaction cards, which is the most stalemate-prone configuration this design can have. The key points and the turn cap are the only things pulling the armies into contact.

If the first runs show both sides hiding behind walls and upgrading until the cap, that is not a tuning failure — it is evidence that the key points are too weak to be worth crossing the board for, or that interaction cards are structural rather than flavor and should be pulled forward.

The key points may be too central

The opposite failure. Three free upgrades a turn is a lot in a game where a single raise can cost four or five symbols, and they sit in a row across the middle of a narrow board. If holding the center is simply correct, the game becomes one brawl on row 9 and the deck stops mattering.

Watch whether any game is decided somewhere other than the middle row. If not, the points are too strong, and the dial is their count and placement before their effect.

Investment loss is a cliff, not a slope

Losing a heavily upgraded unit is catastrophic and unrecoverable. This may produce excellent drama or paralytic risk aversion.

The countervailing force is that deck output is independent of how many units you have, so losing units concentrates your upgrades into fewer bodies — three units at attack 2 beat five at attack 1. That is the built-in rubber band, and it is the main reason kill-them-all is viable as a win condition despite being snowbally on paper. It also means over-concentrating into a single super unit loses, since one monster cannot kill five things while five things focus it.

Open questions

The rules are settled. What is left is numbers, and one map.

  1. Card costs and symbol densities. Proposed in The market. The primary balance dial and the least confident part of this document.
  2. Stat lines. Proposed at soldier 6/1/1/4 and archer 4/1/2/3. The movement gap is fixed at one point by the kiting argument; every other value is free, and HP is the one expected to move.
  3. Draft budget. Proposed at three cards for at most 8 coin. Complicated by disjoint pools: a budget that is fine for a mono-type army may be too thin for a mixed one, which is an argument for watching the 3–2 split specifically.
  4. Pile sizes. Proposed at ten. This is the only lever in version 1 that lets one player's buying affect the other's, so it is worth setting deliberately rather than by convention.
Not open — settled since last revision

Key points grant a straight upgrade, not symbols. Key points upgrade the occupying unit. HP, healing, and regeneration are absent from version 1 entirely. The turn cap value is a harness setting rather than a rule. Joint cards and a mirror anti-convergence constraint are both out.

Deferred to later versions

Not rejected, just not in version 1.

Interaction cards

The intended variety layer, and the main reason the card pool can grow without becoming permutations of the same three symbols. Candidates: movement taxes around your units, pinning a unit for a turn, denying upgrades to a target unit, blocking line of sight with smoke, compelling a unit to move toward you, and conventional deck attacks like discard.

Direct damage from cards is excluded on purpose — it would let a player ignore their units and just spam damage.

Joint cards

Cards covering two stats, or usable on either unit type. The natural answer to the deck fragmentation that disjoint pools create, and the only efficient purchase for a mixed army. Held back from version 1 precisely because that fragmentation is the pressure being tested — adding the relief valve before measuring the pressure would tell us nothing.

Terrain manipulation cards

Creating and destroying walls. Cool, but an addition rather than a core mechanic.

Board-conditional symbol cards

Symbols gated on board state. Probably a late diversity addition rather than a core mechanic, and hard to get right — the conditions have to be genuinely restrictive to be interesting.

HP, healing, and regeneration

All three are out of version 1 together, because they only work as a set: max HP needs a way to climb back to it, and healing needs a ceiling to heal toward. The lightest version that makes the set coherent is regeneration without cards — a unit that neither moves nor attacks recovers 1 HP — which would restore meaning to a max HP raise without adding a pile or a shield mechanic.

Worth revisiting only after run 1 shows whether flat HP produces a decent attrition curve on its own.

Promotion

Paying a large pile of symbols to transform a unit into something better. A sink for large turns and a way to give the game a tech arc.

Revival

Restoring a fallen unit at high cost. Held in reserve in case attrition proves too one-directional.

A healer unit type

Cut from version 1 because healing directly opposes the only win path, and a zero-attack unit is a free fifth of the opponent's win condition.

Rejected

Recorded so they do not get re-proposed.

Attachments

Cards that leave the deck to become permanent unit traits. Rejected because an attachment is a one-time conversion of deck value into board value: once played, that card has stopped participating in the game. Enough of them and the deck becomes a launch vehicle you discard, and the game turns into a skirmish game with a deckbuilding prologue.

The general principle this establishes: card effects must be repeatable on draw, not one-time conversions. Every future card idea should be run through that filter.

A trashing card

Superseded rather than rejected on its merits. The free once-per-turn trash does the same job without occupying a supply pile, and in a pool this small every pile has to earn its slot.

Supply pile exhaustion as the clock

Does not measure elapsed game in this design, and can be avoided by a player who stops buying. Replaced by a turn cap.

A shield or defense mechanic

As the answer to max HP. Too strong given how low attack values are.

Healing cards

As the answer to max HP. Too strong, and they work against the win condition.
